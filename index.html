Efficacy and Safety of Baxdrostat in Uncontrolled and Resistant Hypertension (BaxHTN)

Flack JM, Azizi M, Brown JM, Dwyer JP, Fronczek J, Jones ESW, Olsson DS, Perl S, Shibata H, Wang J-G, Wilderäng U, Wittes J, Williams B, for the BaxHTN Investigators.
N Engl J Med 2025;393:1363-74 (published online 30 Aug 2025) · DOI 10.1056/NEJMoa2507109 · ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06034743 · Funder: AstraZeneca
Source file: NEJMoa2507109.pdf (Disease/Blood Pressure)

At a glance

  • Design: Phase 3, multinational (214 sites), randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial; 12-week primary comparison, followed by open-label and randomised-withdrawal phases (52 weeks planned).
  • Population: 796 randomised adults (794 treated) with seated systolic BP (SBP) 140 to <170 mm Hg despite 2 (uncontrolled) or ≥3 (resistant) antihypertensive drugs including a diuretic.
  • Intervention: Baxdrostat 1 mg or 2 mg once daily vs placebo, added to background therapy.
  • Primary result: Placebo-corrected change in seated SBP at week 12: –8.7 mm Hg (1 mg) and –9.8 mm Hg (2 mg); both P<0.001.
  • Main risks: Hyperkalaemia (serum K+ >6.0 mmol/L in 2.3% / 3.0% vs 0.4%), hyponatraemia, early reversible eGFR fall (~–7 ml/min/1.73 m²).

1. Patient population studied

Who was eligible?

  • Men and women aged ≥18 years with hard-to-control hypertension: mean seated office SBP 140 to <170 mm Hg at screening (p.2).
  • BP elevated despite maximally tolerated doses, for ≥4 weeks before screening, of either:
    • 2 antihypertensive drugs = uncontrolled hypertension, or
    • ≥3 antihypertensive drugs = resistant hypertension;
    • in both cases the regimen had to include a diuretic.
  • After a 2-week single-blind placebo run-in, randomisation required seated SBP ≥135 mm Hg and good adherence (direct observation and ≥80% by pill count) (p.2-3).
  • Full inclusion/exclusion criteria (e.g. potassium and eGFR cut-offs, primary aldosteronism) are in the Supplementary Appendix and are not reported in the main article. MRAs and potassium-sparing diuretics were allowed only in the standard-of-care group of the later open-label phase (p.3).

2. Intervention treatment protocol

  • Drug: baxdrostat, a highly selective aldosterone synthase inhibitor (plasma half-life ≈30 h, enabling once-daily dosing) (p.2).
  • Doses (Part 1): 1 mg or 2 mg orally once daily for 12 weeks, added to unchanged background antihypertensive therapy; 1:1:1 randomisation with placebo, stratified by uncontrolled vs resistant hypertension and by baseline seated SBP <145 vs ≥145 mm Hg (p.3).
  • Run-in: 2-week single-blind placebo run-in before randomisation (p.2-3).
  • Background therapy: kept constant during double-blind periods unless seated SBP >170 mm Hg or DBP >105 mm Hg, when rescue medication could be added at the investigator's discretion (p.3).
  • Monitoring: monthly visits (BP taken before the daily dose; Microlife WatchBP Office 2G); blinded central-lab measurement of creatinine, sodium, aldosterone, plasma renin activity and baxdrostat levels; potassium measured at local and central labs. 24-h ambulatory BP at baseline and week 12 at 34 sites (p.3).
  • Four-part design (52 weeks planned):
    PartPeriodContent
    1Weeks 0–12Double-blind: baxdrostat 1 mg, 2 mg or placebo (primary comparison)
    2Weeks 12–24Open-label: prior 2 mg continued; prior 1 mg and prior placebo re-randomised to 2 mg baxdrostat or standard of care (4:1 and 1:4)
    3Weeks 24–32Double-blind randomised withdrawal: patients on 2 mg re-randomised 2:1 to continue 2 mg or switch to placebo (cohort 1 only)
    4Weeks 32–52Open-label 2 mg, further safety data (still ongoing at data cutoff; not reported)
  • Potassium-triggered interruption/stopping algorithms and titration rules are not described in the main article (Supplementary Appendix).

3. Control group treatment protocol

  • Placebo once daily for 12 weeks after the same 2-week placebo run-in, on top of the same unchanged background antihypertensive therapy (p.1-3).
  • Same monthly visit schedule, laboratory monitoring and rescue thresholds (SBP >170 / DBP >105 mm Hg). Rescue medication in part 1: placebo 7 patients (3%) vs 3 (1%) in each baxdrostat group (p.4).
  • Part 3 (withdrawal): the placebo arm consists of patients previously treated with 2 mg baxdrostat for 24 weeks who were switched to placebo for 8 weeks – this is a withdrawal comparison, not a continuation of the original placebo group.
  • In part 2, "standard of care" (background therapy, with MRAs/potassium-sparing diuretics allowed) served as a comparator for longer-term safety; its composition is not detailed.

Other findings

  • Time course: substantial BP lowering was visible by week 4 with further lowering thereafter (Figure 1; numerical values not printed).
  • Hormones: aldosterone fell and plasma renin activity rose with baxdrostat; neither fully returned to baseline after withdrawal. Numerical values and ambulatory-BP results (24-h and night-time SBP; exploratory, 34 sites) are only in the Supplement.
  • Sensitivity analyses produced similar results (Table S6, not included in the main text).

Key safety observations

  • Most common AEs: hyperkalaemia, hyponatraemia, hypotension, muscle spasms and dizziness; most events were mild (counts in Table S9, not in the main text).
  • Hyperkalaemia: the main class-related risk. Central-lab K+ >6.0 mmol/L in 2.3% / 3.0% vs 0.4%; only 3 patients (1.1%) in each baxdrostat group were confirmed >6.0 mmol/L by a same-day local lab. Changes occurred mostly in the first 2 weeks and then remained stable.
  • Hyponatraemia: Na+ <135 mmol/L in 19.1% (1 mg) / 22.8% (2 mg) vs 7.0% (placebo); Na+ <132 mmol/L in 5.7% / 8.0% vs 1.1%. Also mostly within the first 2 weeks.
  • Kidney function: mean eGFR change at week 12: –7.0 ± 12.8 (1 mg), –6.9 ± 12.4 (2 mg) vs –0.1 ± 8.6 (placebo) ml/min/1.73 m². eGFR change ≥30% in 12.6% / 15.6% vs 1.5%; ≥50% in 1.5% / 1.1% vs 0.4%. The fall occurred early, stabilised on continued 2 mg and returned toward baseline after withdrawal. Two investigator-defined acute kidney injury events (one placebo, one 2 mg).
  • Serious AEs possibly related to baxdrostat: 1 hyperkalaemia (1 mg group) and 2 hyponatraemia (one per baxdrostat group).
  • Adrenal: no cases of adrenal insufficiency reported. Cortisol/ACTH data, sexual/endocrine adverse effects and MACE counts are not reported in the main article.
  • Long-term safety (52-week data) was not yet available; part 4 was ongoing.

6. Discussion of the study

Authors' interpretation

  • Adding baxdrostat to background therapy lowered seated SBP by about 9–10 mm Hg beyond placebo within 12 weeks. A reduction of this magnitude is, according to prior evidence cited by the authors, associated with lower cardiovascular and mortality risk – but BaxHTN itself did not measure clinical outcomes.
  • The consistent effect across uncontrolled and resistant hypertension, and across age, sex, ethnicity, region and BMI, suggests that dysregulated aldosterone contributes broadly to hard-to-control hypertension, possibly including aldosterone "breakthrough" despite renin–angiotensin-system blockade.
  • Aldosterone suppression with rising renin may reflect additional sodium excretion despite background diuretics (a mechanistic hypothesis that was not formally tested).
  • The randomised withdrawal showed only a slow, small rebound (+1.4 mm Hg over 8 weeks) even though the drug is cleared within about 1 week – consistent with a durable effect on sodium handling, vascular and/or sympathetic mechanisms – and supports that the BP effect is drug-related.
  • Safety was described as consistent with lorundrostat trials (e.g. Launch-HTN: –9.1 mm Hg at week 6). The early eGFR dip with recovery after withdrawal was interpreted as a haemodynamic (renal perfusion) effect.
  • Limitations named by the authors: ambulatory BP available in only a small subset; women and Black patients under-represented relative to the real world (Black patients 7% overall vs 36% in North America); adherence was not objectively measured throughout (although baxdrostat plasma levels suggested good adherence, ≤10% below quantification).

Critical appraisal (reviewer's perspective, based on the article)

  • Strengths: large phase 3 trial; double-blind, placebo-controlled with run-in and adherence checks; two doses; real-world-style population including both uncontrolled and resistant hypertension; consistent subgroup direction; withdrawal phase adds causal support; relevant independent data monitoring committee.
  • Effect size in context: the placebo-corrected effect (~9–10 mm Hg) is clinically meaningful, but the placebo arm also dropped by ~6 mm Hg; the dose-response between 1 mg and 2 mg was small (≈1 mm Hg), which may make 1 mg an attractive option when hyperkalaemia risk matters (hyperkalaemia needing intervention: 2.7% vs 7.9%).
  • Surrogate endpoint and duration: 12-week BP data; no evidence yet on cardiovascular, renal or mortality outcomes. Long-term (52-week) data are pending.
  • Safety needs close monitoring: the hyperkalaemia and hyponatraemia signals, and the early eGFR fall (≥30% change in about 1 in 7 patients on 2 mg), imply laboratory monitoring early after initiation – similar to MRA practice. The trial population had largely preserved kidney function (mean eGFR ~85), so extrapolation to advanced CKD, older frail patients or patients on ACE-inhibitor/ARB + other potassium-raising drugs requires caution.
  • Comparators: no head-to-head comparison with spironolactone (the guideline-recommended fourth-line drug), so relative efficacy and tolerability versus MRAs remain unknown.
  • Reporting and sponsor: the trial was designed with, and data collected and analysed by, the sponsor (AstraZeneca), with statistical analyses verified by two internal teams; authors had access to the data. Many details (exclusion criteria, ambulatory BP, hormone values, potassium stopping rules) sit in the supplement. Endpoint denominators (263 vs 264 placebo) were not explained.
  • Take-home: baxdrostat is an efficacious, once-daily add-on option for uncontrolled/resistant hypertension with a manageable but real electrolyte and renal-function monitoring burden; its place relative to spironolactone and its long-term outcome benefit still need to be established.
